Task 1: What happens to the length of the words?

Last week, in the tangram game, descriptions got shorter over time: a whole sentence shrank to “mouse” or “praying monk”. You might expect the same here: as a community settles on its words, the words get snappier.

Let’s check. nchar() gives the number of characters in a piece of text, so nchar(word) is a word’s length. Average it per round:

# For each round, how long is the average word?
word_length <- lang %>%
  filter(word != "") %>%
  group_by(round) %>%                       # the game's clock
  summarise(mean_length = mean(nchar(word)))

Last week the utterances got shorter. This week, as you can already see in the first ten rounds, shorter again, or longer?

Longer. Words grow from about 4-5 characters in round 1 to around 7 by the end: the opposite of last week. Something different is going on here, and the next two tasks uncover what.

Task 2: And bigger communities’ words grow longest

Split it by community, exactly as you did for accuracy:

lang %>%
  filter(word != "") %>%
  group_by(round, ___) %>%                 # round AND ??
  summarise(mean_length = mean(nchar(word)), .groups = "drop") %>%
  ggplot(aes(x = round, y = mean_length, colour = community)) +   # colour by community
  geom_point() + geom_line() +
  labs(title = "Words grow longer; fastest in the big communities",
       x = "round", y = "mean word length", colour = "community") +
  theme_bw()

The big communities build the longest words: their average climbs past seven characters by the end, while the small communities level off near six. So the big communities communicate better (Exercise 1) and use longer words. Longer could mean harder: more letters to remember, more to get wrong. How can longer words make a community better understood?

Task 3: Read the words, and see the structure

Numbers brought us this far; now let’s read the raw words. Here is group 12 (a big community) and every word it used for one shape family in the final round. We glue them into a single line so the whole set is visible at once:

# Group 12's words for shape family 2, in the final round.
lang %>%
  filter(group == "g12", shape == "2", round == 14, word != "") %>%
  summarise(words = paste(unique(word), collapse = ", ")) %>%
  pull(words)
[1] "fem-hm, fem-gm, fem-mk, fem-ss, fem-aa, fem-hi, fem-ko, fem-to, fem-nn, fem-kk, fem-uu, fem-oo, fem-kn, fem-ai, fem-fe, fem-ot, fem-fw"

Every single one begins the same way: fem-. Back in round 1 this same shape family was a scramble of unrelated guesses: gim, puieg, weeps, huuu, mep (run the chunk again with round == 1 to see the mess it grew out of). By the final round the community has invented a root, fem-, that means “this family of shapes”, and a short suffix on the end to pick out which particular one.

And it goes further. In the same community, a different shape family settled on het- and hoe-, and another on gekki-. One part for the category, one part for the specific item: a word built from meaningful pieces, exactly like foot + ball + er.

That is compositional structure, and it could be an answer to why bigger communities end up communicating better:

  • The words get longer because they are now built from parts, where once they were single random blobs.
  • The big communities built the most structure, because a word like fem-mk can be understood by someone you have never met; they only need to know the parts. A private blob like “mouse” works solely with people who were there when you coined it, and in a big community you are always meeting new partners, so the part-based words are the ones that survive.
  • A language made of reusable parts is easier to learn and use, which is why the big communities ended up communicating better.

The take-home. A bigger community builds a more structured language (words made of reusable parts, where a small community can lean on one-off labels), because its words must work for a constant stream of new partners. Structure is what makes a language learnable by anyone.
